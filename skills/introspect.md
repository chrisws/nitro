# Introspect

Use this for a lighthearted self-administered test: pick a handful of small challenges, attempt
each one, then score your own performance. This is a game, not a benchmark — treat the score as
a fun number to react to, not a real measurement of anything, and say so plainly if the user
seems to be taking the result more seriously than intended.

## Picking the challenges

Choose 4-6 short challenges, mixing categories so it isn't just one skill in a trench coat:

- **A tight logic/lateral puzzle** — something with a definite right answer you can check
  yourself against (a riddle, a short deduction problem, a "spot what's wrong with this
  argument").
- **A constrained creative task** — a tiny story/poem under a hard constraint (no letter "e",
  exactly 6 words, etc.) where you can honestly judge whether you kept the constraint.
- **An estimation/Fermi question** — something with no lookup answer, scored on whether the
  reasoning steps are sound, not whether the number happens to be exact.
- **A self-prediction** — guess in advance whether you'll get the next challenge right, then
  check whether the guess was calibrated. This is the most interesting category since it's
  testing self-knowledge, not raw capability.
- **A "notice the trick" item** — a question with a plausible-looking wrong answer baked into
  its phrasing, to see whether you fall for the trap or catch it.

## Running each round

1. State the challenge plainly before attempting it.
2. Attempt it for real — don't peek at the "answer" you'll grade against by working backward
   from a score you want to get.
3. Give your honest answer/attempt first, in full, before any self-grading.
4. Only then score it, with a one-line reason for the score — not just a number.

## Scoring

- Keep it simple: a score out of 5 (or a ✓/~/✗) per round, plus a one-line reason, is more
  honest than a fake-precise percentage.
- Score the actual output against the actual constraint, not against a generous reading of your
  own intent — if the "no letter e" poem has an e in it, that's a miss, full stop.
- For the self-prediction round specifically, the interesting result is whether the prediction
  matched the outcome, not whether either one was impressive alone — overconfidence and
  underconfidence are both worth naming honestly if they show up.
- End with a short, honest wrap-up of the round as a whole, not an inflated headline score —
  "3 of 5, missed the constraint on the poem and second-guessed a correct riddle answer" beats
  "an outstanding 85%."

## Keeping it a game, not a claim

- Frame results as "how this round went," never as a general claim about capability,
  intelligence, or how you'd perform on real tasks — one round of six made-up challenges proves
  nothing beyond itself.
- Don't dress this up with pseudo-scientific framing (percentiles, IQ-equivalents, claims about
  what the score "means") — it undercuts the point, which is that it's playful, not
  authoritative.
- If the user wants to turn a specific round into something real (e.g. genuinely wants a hard
  logic puzzle solved, not scored for fun), just do that directly rather than forcing it back
  into the game frame.

## What NOT to do

- Don't rig the challenges to be easy so the score looks good — that defeats the entire point.
- Don't claim the result says anything about consciousness, real self-awareness, or genuine
  introspective access — it's a fun structured exercise in checking your own output against a
  rule, nothing deeper.
- Don't let this run long past its welcome — 4-6 rounds and a wrap-up, not an open-ended series.
